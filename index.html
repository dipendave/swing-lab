Set the seam, the ball and the day. Watch what the air does.

You have six balls against a batter of medium ability. Wides and no-balls cost a run and are bowled again. Your aim is good, not perfect: each ball lands close to where you set it, not exactly on it.

A fast bowler lets go of a ball that weighs about 160 grams, and for half a second nothing touches it. Yet it bends, late, sometimes by more than the width of the stumps. Bowlers were doing that long before anyone could explain it, and it is still one of the best things to watch in any sport.

Swing Lab is a fun project, built to see how much of that magic falls out of real aerodynamics. Tilt the seam, scuff one side, change the weather, and watch the ball do what the greats made it do. It is a toy for anyone who has watched a replay and asked how the ball did that.

The bowlers who made it an art
  • Sarfraz Nawaz An early master of making an old ball swing the wrong way. Took 7 wickets for 1 run in one spell at Melbourne in 1979, and passed the craft on to Imran Khan, who passed it on again.
  • Kapil Dev A natural outswinger from India's greatest fast-bowling all-rounder. Finished with 434 Test wickets, the world record at the time.
  • Wasim Akram Left arm, either direction, new ball or old. Bowled Allan Lamb and Chris Lewis with consecutive balls in the 1992 World Cup final, one swinging each way.
  • Waqar Younis The reverse-swinging yorker at full pace, curving in at the toes.
  • James Anderson 704 Test wickets, the most by any fast bowler, built on moving a new ball both ways.
  • Jasprit Bumrah The first bowler to reach 200 Test wickets at an average under 20. Took 6 for 45 with reverse swing at Visakhapatnam in 2024, including a yorker that bowled Ollie Pope.
  • Anya Shrubsole Inswing that won a World Cup: 6 for 46 at Lord's in 2017, the best figures in a Women's World Cup final.
  • Bob Massie 16 wickets on Test debut at Lord's in 1972, hooping it under heavy skies.

    How the physics works

    A cricket ball swings because the thin layer of air hugging its surface lets go of the two sides at different places. Everything here follows from working out where that happens.

    The air

    The surface is divided into 32 strips running from the nose of the ball to its wake. On each strip the layer starts smooth (laminar). It turns turbulent if the leather is rough enough for the speed, using Achenbach's rough-sphere measurements, or if it crosses the seam fast enough for the stitches to trip it. A laminar layer leaves the ball about 82° round from the nose. A turbulent one clings to roughly 120°, less if it was tripped early, has run over scuffed leather, or was already turbulent when it hit the seam.

    The pressure those separation lines imply is integrated over the ball to give drag and side force. Conventional swing, reverse swing and contrast swing are not separate rules. They are the same calculation at different speeds and surface conditions.

    The flight

    The ball is a spinning rigid body. If the seam is not square to the spin axis it wobbles, and the forces are recomputed from where the seam actually is every two milliseconds. Backspin adds Magnus lift. Wind changes the airspeed the ball feels.

    The pitch

    The bounce is an impulse calculation with friction. A fast ball skids the whole way through contact. A slow one can bite and leave rolling. When the seam is on the ground it resists sliding across itself more than along itself, which is what moves the ball off the seam.

    The weather, honestly

    Humid air is slightly thinner than dry air, so humidity alone does almost nothing; that matches wind-tunnel work. Temperature and altitude change air density and viscosity, which shifts the speed at which swing reverses. Cloud is modelled the way James, MacDonald and Hart suggested in 2012: sunshine on the outfield stirs up small-scale turbulence that disturbs the laminar side, and cloud calms it. That last effect is a hypothesis, so it is kept small.

    What it is calibrated to

    Peak side force about 0.3 of the dynamic pressure on the ball's cross-section at around 108 km/h with a 20° seam (Bentley and others 1982, Mehta 2005). A new ball reversing only above roughly 140 km/h. Contrast swing changing direction near 115 km/h. Drag near 0.5 at low speed.

    The batter, in challenge mode

    The batter is not a dice roll. They read the length early and choose where to meet the ball, then commit about a fifth of a second before it arrives. From that instant they expect it to carry on as it is going: part of the swing they have already seen, and a plain bounce. The bat goes where that expectation says, pulled a little toward the truth if there is time after the ball pitches, and blurred by ordinary human error. Then the real ball arrives where the physics puts it.

    The gap between ball and bat decides the rest: middle, edge or miss, and which edge. So late swing finds the edge, a good length beats the bat because the ball pitches after the batter has committed, and a short ball is easier to line up. Bowled, caught and leg before wicket follow from where the ball actually went, and leg before follows the law: not out if it pitched outside leg stump, or struck the pad outside the line with a shot offered.

    What is judged, not simulated: how many runs a clean hit is worth, and whether an edge carries and is held. Those are drawn from tables that depend on the stroke and the field. There is no running between the wickets, so no run-outs, and no byes or leg byes.

    The players' bodies are a real human model, the open one from the MakeHuman project, dressed in whites. Their movements are worked out by formula, not motion-captured. The bowler runs in, lands back foot then front foot, and brings a straight arm over so that the hand is exactly where the ball's flight begins. The bat is put where the stroke needs it, the hands stay on the handle, and bodies lean and step to reach, without any bone changing length or a planted foot sliding. Fielders walk in, watch the ball and go after it, but who fields it and how many are run is still decided by the tables above, not by the chase you see.

    The light is a daylight model: sky colour from the scattering of sunlight in air, the sun's shadows, and the sky itself as the fill. Cloud cover dims the sun and humidity thickens the haze. It is there to look right; none of it feeds back into the physics.

    What it leaves out

    No aerodynamic torque on the seam, so the wobble you set is the wobble you get. No moisture on the ball itself. Pitch behaviour is a reasoned mapping from hardness, grass, moisture and wear onto bounce and friction, not a measurement of any particular ground.
